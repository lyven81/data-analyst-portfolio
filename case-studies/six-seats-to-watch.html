The Hung State Assembly

How one seat decides who governs Negeri Sembilan

Negeri Sembilan votes on 1 August 2026 to fill 36 state-assembly seats. This forecast, written before the vote, works out who is likely to win each seat and, above all, where the result is actually decided. It uses only what is knowable in advance: the candidates standing in each seat, the last two state elections, and the make-up of the electorate.

Three forces contest the state. Pakatan Harapan (PH) stands in every seat. Barisan Nasional and Perikatan Nasional (BN-PN) have shared out the seats between them, so only one of them stands in each seat, and a third Malay contender, Bersatu, stands separately in 24 of the 36. The government turns on six Malay-majority seats: Ampangan, Klawang, Labu, Pilah, Sikamat and Paroi. With the non-PH vote split in five of them, the result is a hung assembly of 18 seats each, and a single seat, Labu, decides whether anyone reaches a majority.

How a seat is settled or contested

Each seat starts as a two-way score: PH against the stronger of the BN-PN pact. PH's score is the share of the vote it won in 2023. The pact score pools BN and PN together, keeping most of the smaller partner's vote, since only one of them stands in each seat.

The gap between the two scores still sets the seat. When one side leads by fifteen points or more, the seat is settled, too far ahead for a normal swing to close. When the gap is under fifteen points, the seat is a contest, close enough to fall either way.

Across all 36 seats, 30 come out settled and six stay close. The settled 30 split BN-PN 17 and PH 13, so neither side reaches the 19 needed to govern on safe seats alone. In the six close seats, where three candidates stand, the winner is the one with the most votes, not a majority, so the deciding question is how the non-PH vote divides between the pact candidate and Bersatu.

The forecast, seat by seat

1. How is the forecast built?

The forecast is built in four plain steps. First, describe each seat: how it voted in 2023, and who lives there, from the census. Second, read the candidate list and record who actually stands in each seat, coalition by coalition. Third, call a winner in each seat: where two stand, the higher score wins; where three stand, split the non-PH vote between the pact candidate and Bersatu and take the plurality leader. Fourth, record how sure the method is in each seat, so the close calls stand apart from the safe ones.

Its main assumption is a single question: how much of the non-PH Malay vote goes to Bersatu rather than the pact candidate. A larger Bersatu share favours PH on a plurality; a smaller one keeps the pact ahead. That split is the biggest source of doubt and is built into the range below.

2. What does the forecast project?

The projected seats: a hung assembly, 18 each, with 19 needed for a majority.

The central forecast is PH 18 and BN-PN 18: a hung assembly. PH reaches 18 from 13 settled seats plus five of the six contests; the BN-PN pact reaches 18 from 17 settled seats plus one contest. Neither crosses the 19-seat line. One seat, Labu, is the difference between a majority and a tie: it is the single contest whose result decides whether anyone governs outright.

3. Which seats are already safe?

Thirty seats are settled by a comfortable margin, and they split into two floors. The BN-PN pact is safe in 17 seats, its Malay heartland. PH is safe in 13 seats, its non-Malay and mixed strongholds. Because 19 is needed to govern, neither floor is a majority on its own: the pact floor is two short, the PH floor is six short.

BN-PN safe (17): Rantau, Pertang, Johol, Chembong, Bagan Pinang, Gemencheh, Gemas, Linggi, Senaling, Jeram Padang, Seri Menanti, Serting, Sungai Lui, Palong, Kota, Juasseh, Lenggeng
PH safe (13): Lobak, Mambau, Bukit Kepayang, Chuah, Seremban Jaya, Lukut, Bahau, Rahang, Nilai, Repah, Temiang, Sri Tanjung, Chennah

Bersatu stands in most of the safe Malay seats too, which narrows the pact's winning margins there. In the central forecast it does not change who holds them: those margins run from the twenties into the nineties, too wide for a split to overturn. These 30 are not where the election is won; they set the starting line.

4. What decides a seat?

Make-up sorts the map: PH holds the non-Malay seats, BN-PN the Malay seats.

The make-up of a seat sorts the map. PH wins almost every non-Malay and mixed seat; the BN-PN pact wins almost every Malay-majority seat. This two-sided lock is why 30 seats are settled: most sit clearly on one side of the divide. A third Malay candidate cracks that lock. In the Malay-majority seats that are only just safe for the pact, Bersatu divides the non-PH vote, and a plurality contest is easier to lose than a two-way one.

The contest survives, and in places deepens, where the divide is blurred and a third candidate is on the ballot. That is where the six are, and it is why five of them are now three-cornered.

5. The six seats to watch

These are the seats the method is least sure about: Malay-majority seats where the gap between PH and the pact is under fifteen points. Five of the six now carry a third candidate.

PH already leads four of the six (Ampangan, Klawang, Pilah, Sikamat), so a third candidate only makes them safer. Labu is the pivot: the member who won it in 2023 now stands as Bersatu, while BN fields a separate candidate, dividing the pact vote between an established local name and a new one. It takes only a fifteen-point share of the pact vote for the pact's plurality to fall below PH, and a sitting member usually carries far more than that. Paroi is more resistant: the PN member won by more than five thousand votes in 2023, so Bersatu would have to take almost a quarter of the pact vote to pull it to PH.

6. Does Bersatu win a seat, or only split the vote?

Bersatu stands in 24 seats but is placed to win at most one. In the five contests where it divides the non-PH vote it is a new name without a local base, so it lowers the pact's plurality and passes the seat to PH rather than taking it. In the safe Malay seats the established BN or PN margins are too wide for a first-time candidate to overtake. The single exception is Labu, where the 2023 winner now stands under the Bersatu banner and carries a personal vote, turning the seat into a straight fight between PH and Bersatu.

That one seat is what separates a spoiler from a kingmaker. If Labu falls to PH, Bersatu wins nothing and its whole effect is the split that levels the assembly at 18 each: a party that changes who governs without governing. If the sitting member holds Labu, the count becomes PH 17, the pact 18 and Bersatu 1, and in a chamber where 19 is a majority that single seat is the one that lets the pact reach 19. Either way Bersatu's own haul is zero or one seat, and its weight is large as a splitter and small as a seat-winner.

What the six decide

7. Do the six decide it, or is it foregone?

They decide it, and the maths is no longer one-sided. Neither bloc reaches 19 on its settled seats, so the six carry the government. The pact holds 17 safe seats and needs two of the six; it is now favoured in only one of them, Paroi. PH holds 13 safe seats and needs all six; it is now favoured in five, one short of the clean sweep that a bare majority would require.

No side is a clear favourite once the third candidate is on the ballot: the central case is a hung assembly, and the split in two seats decides whether it tips to PH or back to the pact.

So neither bloc reaches 19. The pact, favoured in only one of the six, lands on 18, one short of a majority; PH, favoured in five, also lands on 18. The result is a tie, and whether it breaks toward a majority turns on how the split falls in Labu and Paroi.

8. Who is likely to govern?

The forecast points to a hung assembly with PH holding the plurality edge. PH is favoured in five of the six deciding seats and anchored by non-Malay and mixed seats it cannot be dislodged from, which puts it level with the pact and within one seat of a majority. The BN-PN pact is favoured in its heartland and in Paroi, but its path to 19 now depends on holding Labu against a split it is unlikely to hold. The state's next government is decided less by a broad statewide mood than by how a third Malay candidate divides the vote in six seats.

What this forecast can and cannot say

The forecast is confident about the shape of the result: the two floors are secure, PH owns the non-Malay seats, the pact owns the Malay heartland, and the government is decided in six Malay-majority seats that are now mostly three-cornered. It gives the exact seat count as a range rather than a single number, because that rests on how far Bersatu splits the non-PH vote in those six, which is not knowable before the vote. Read the hung central case and the PH plurality edge as the firm finding, and the final margin as a range from BN-PN 19-17 to PH 19-17.

Working hypotheses

This forecast rests on three rules the 1 August result will test. First, make-up still locks most seats: PH holds the non-Malay and mixed seats, the pact holds the Malay heartland, and that alone settles 30 of 36 whatever the candidate field. Second, the Malay-majority seats are the whole battleground: every seat still in play is Malay-majority, and the government is decided there. Third, the split, not a swing, is the decisive mechanic: a third Malay candidate dividing the non-PH vote hands plurality seats to PH, and where that candidate is a sitting member, as in Labu, the split is large enough to move the seat.

Recommendations

To forecast an election with data, start by counting seats, not the popular vote. The map decides who governs, and a bloc can trail the statewide vote yet still win it. So find each side's safe floor first, and check whether either already reaches a majority.

Then count the candidates before pooling them. A merger assumed before nominations can dissolve on nomination day, and a single extra candidate in a close seat turns a two-way call into a plurality one. Read every seat on the short list as open, model the vote split rather than a clean transfer, and treat the size of that split as the biggest source of doubt. And publish the forecast, with its contested-seat list, before the vote.

Method and data

Each seat was scored from the 2023 vote and the 2020 census, PH against the stronger of the BN-PN pact. A seat is settled when that lead is fifteen points or more, and a contest when it is less. The candidate list sets the field in each seat. Where a third candidate stands, the seat is called on a plurality: PH wins once the pact's share, reduced by the Bersatu split, falls below PH's. The split needed to flip each contest is reported as a share of the pact vote, so the range is reproducible from the published scores.

The data is real: candidate results from the Malaysian Election Corpus, census figures from 2020, and the 2026 nomination list, all on the current boundaries. The forecast reads past results and the candidate field, not polls, so its main risk is how the non-PH vote divides between the pact candidate and Bersatu. This is educational analysis of public election and census data, not advocacy, and it will be checked against the actual result after 1 August 2026.
