Six Seats to Watch

Forecasting the Negeri Sembilan state election

Negeri Sembilan votes on 1 August 2026 to fill 36 state-assembly seats. This forecast, written before the vote, works out who is likely to win each seat and, more importantly, where the result will actually be decided. It uses only what is knowable in advance: the last two state elections and the make-up of each seat.

The contest has narrowed to two blocs. Barisan Nasional (BN) is contesting alongside Perikatan Nasional (PN) as a single grand coalition, against Pakatan Harapan (PH). Thirty of the 36 seats are already settled and split almost evenly, so the government comes down to six Malay-majority seats: Ampangan, Klawang, Labu, Pilah, Sikamat and Paroi. The BN-PN bloc is the favourite, but its majority rides entirely on that handful.

How a seat is settled or contested

Each seat is scored as a two-way match: PH against the combined BN-PN bloc. PH's score is the share of the vote it won in 2023. The BN-PN score pools BN and PN together, keeping most of the smaller partner's vote, since a merged ticket loses a few supporters.

The gap between the two scores is what settles the seat. When one side leads by fifteen points or more, the seat is settled, too far ahead for a normal swing to close. When the gap is under fifteen points, the seat is a contest, close enough to fall either way.

Across all 36 seats, 30 come out settled and six stay close. The settled 30 split BN-PN 17 and PH 13, so neither side reaches the 19 needed to govern. That leaves the six close seats to decide who gets there and forms the next government.

The forecast, before the vote

1. How is the forecast built?

The forecast is built in four plain steps. First, describe each seat: how it voted in 2023, and who lives there, from the census. Second, treat BN and PN as one bloc, since they are contesting together, and combine their vote where they no longer stand against each other. Third, set that combined bloc against PH in every seat to call a winner. Fourth, record how sure the method is in each seat, so the close calls stand apart from the safe ones.

Its main assumption is that voters largely follow their coalitions once BN and PN pool their support. Where that transfer is imperfect the bloc's margins narrow, which is built into the six contests below.

2. What does the forecast project?

The projected seats: a one-seat BN-PN majority, 19 to 17.

On the pre-election evidence, the BN-PN bloc wins 19 of the 36 seats and PH 17, a majority of one. The bloc reaches 19 from 17 settled seats plus two of the six contests; PH reaches 17 from 13 settled seats plus four contests. The result is close, and every seat above the majority line comes from the six.

3. Which seats are already safe?

Thirty seats are settled by a comfortable margin, and they split into two floors. The BN-PN bloc is safe in 17 seats, its Malay heartland. PH is safe in 13 seats, its non-Malay and mixed strongholds. Because 19 is needed to govern, neither floor is a majority on its own: the BN-PN floor is two short, the PH floor is six short.

BN-PN safe (17): Rantau, Pertang, Johol, Chembong, Bagan Pinang, Gemencheh, Gemas, Linggi, Senaling, Jeram Padang, Seri Menanti, Serting, Sungai Lui, Palong, Kota, Juasseh, Lenggeng
PH safe (13): Lobak, Mambau, Bukit Kepayang, Chuah, Seremban Jaya, Lukut, Bahau, Rahang, Nilai, Repah, Temiang, Sri Tanjung, Chennah

These 30 are not where the election is won. They set the starting line; the finish is in the six seats neither bloc has locked.

4. What decides a seat?

Make-up sorts the map: PH holds the non-Malay seats, BN-PN the Malay seats.

The make-up of a seat sorts the map. PH wins almost every non-Malay and mixed seat; the BN-PN bloc wins almost every Malay-majority seat. This two-sided lock is why 30 seats are settled: most sit clearly on one side of the divide. There is only one Chinese-majority seat in the whole state, and the larger-than-usual Indian vote sits inside the non-Malay and mixed seats that PH already holds.

The contest survives only where the divide is blurred, in the Malay-majority seats that are not quite lopsided enough to be safe. That is where the six are.

5. The six seats to watch

These are the seats the method is least sure about: Malay-majority seats where the gap between PH and the BN-PN bloc is under fifteen points. They are where the government will be won or lost.

What the six decide

6. Do the six decide it, or is it foregone?

It is not foregone. Neither bloc reaches a majority on its 30 settled seats, so the six carry the government. But the maths is one-sided. The BN-PN bloc, on 17 safe seats, needs only two of the six to reach 19, and it is favoured in two of them. PH, on 13 safe seats, would have to win all six, including overturning the two seats that lean BN-PN, just to reach a bare majority of its own.

The BN-PN bloc is the clear favourite, but its majority is not locked: it rests entirely on these six seats.

So the six decide two things: whether the BN-PN bloc secures its majority, and how large it is. If the bloc holds its two and takes one or two of PH's close seats, the majority grows comfortable. If PH defends all four of its Malay seats and steals the bloc's two, the majority is denied and the result is level.

7. Who is likely to govern?

The BN-PN bloc is favoured to form the next Negeri Sembilan government, on a bare majority built out of the Malay-majority seats. PH is favoured to be the opposition, anchored by the non-Malay and mixed seats it cannot be dislodged from but unable to reach a majority through them alone. The state's next government is decided less by a broad statewide mood than by six Malay-majority seats where the two blocs run close.

What this forecast can and cannot say

The forecast is confident about the shape of the result: the two floors are secure, PH owns the non-Malay seats, the BN-PN bloc owns the Malay heartland, and the government is decided in six seats. It gives the exact seat count as a range rather than a fixed number, because that rests on how those six break and on how cleanly BN and PN voters transfer to a single candidate, neither knowable before the vote. Read the favourite and the battleground as firm, and the final margin as a range.

Working hypotheses

This forecast rests on three rules the 1 August result will test. First, make-up locks most seats: PH holds the non-Malay and mixed seats, the BN-PN bloc holds the Malay heartland, and that alone settles 30 of 36. Second, the Malay-majority seats are the whole battleground: every seat still in play is Malay-majority, and the government is decided there. Third, pooling the vote is the BN-PN edge: by fielding one candidate where they once split, BN and PN convert narrow three-way losses into wins, and a poor transfer between them would blunt this, the bloc's main risk.

Recommendations

To forecast an election with data, start by counting seats, not the popular vote. The map decides who governs, and a bloc can trail the statewide vote yet still win it. So find each side's safe floor first, and check whether either already reaches a majority.

If neither does, the election lives in the seats left over. Name that short list up front, and read every seat on it as open. And when two parties pool their vote, forecast the combined bloc, treating an imperfect transfer between them as the biggest source of doubt.

Method and data

Each seat was treated as a two-way race, PH against a combined BN-PN candidate. The scores come from the 2023 vote and the 2020 census, with BN and PN pooled behind the stronger of the two. A seat counts as settled when the lead is fifteen points or more, and a contest when it is less.

The data is real: candidate results from the Malaysian Election Corpus and census figures from 2020, all on the current boundaries. The forecast reads past results, not polls, so its main risk is how cleanly BN and PN voters move behind one candidate. It will be checked against the actual result after 1 August 2026.
