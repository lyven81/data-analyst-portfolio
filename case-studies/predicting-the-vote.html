Predicting the Vote

A Johor election forecast tested against reality

Data Stories LabAnalyst report56 seats · Johor 2026
Analyst ReportDashboard
⌂ Home

This case study answers one question for anyone who wants to forecast a Malaysian state election: can a model built only from past results and local demographics call the seats before the vote, and how far can its answer be trusted? It uses the Johor state election of 11 July 2026, 56 seats already decided, as a test bed. The real aim is to judge whether the same method is ready to forecast Negeri Sembilan on 1 August 2026.

The report follows a strict order so the forecast can be judged on its own terms. The forecast comes first, using only information available before polling day, and the model was fitted on the 2018 and 2022 elections without ever seeing the 2026 result. Only then is it scored against what actually happened. The finding is clear: the method names the winner of most seats accurately, is honest about the few it cannot call, and is weaker on the margin of victory, saying who wins more reliably than by how much.

Part 1 · The forecast, before the vote

1. How is the forecast built?

The forecast is built in four plain steps. First, describe each seat: how it voted last time, and who lives there, from the census. Second, learn from history how those factors turned into wins when Johor moved from 2018 to 2022. Third, apply that learned pattern to each seat's 2022 numbers to work out each side's chance of winning, and call the seat for whoever is ahead. Fourth, record how sure the method is in each seat, so the close calls stand apart from the safe ones.

The method's engine is the assumption that the recent swing continues. It carries the 2018-to-2022 movement one step further. If Johor's mood in 2026 turns calmer or sharper than that, the forecast will drift.

2. What does the forecast project?

The projected 2026 seats against the 2022 starting point.

On the pre-election evidence, the method projects BN to remain the largest coalition with 48 of the 56 seats, PH on 6, PN on 1, and one seat to a small party standing alone. This is the 2022 picture with the swing carried one step further.

The projection is only as strong as the confidence behind it. The next question is how much of it the method is actually sure about.

3. Which seats is the forecast unsure about, and why?

The method is highly confident in 42 of the 56 seats. It flags the other 14 as genuine contests, and six of those are true toss-ups. What makes a seat a contest comes from two things the method can see. The first is a mixed community make-up: the contested seats are 37% Chinese on average against 26% for the safe ones, and ten of the fourteen sit in the mixed 30-to-50 percent Chinese band, where neither the Malay nor the Chinese vote settles the outcome. The second is a starting position that the expected swing brings to the tipping point. When a seat lands near fifty-fifty, the method lowers its confidence rather than pretend to a certainty it does not have.

Part 2 · The comparison, after the vote

4. Did the forecast call the winners?

Forecast seats against the actual result, by coalition. The actual result was BN 48, PH 8, PN 0.

Yes, on direction. The forecast called 53 of the 56 seats correctly, about 95 in every 100, and matched BN's seat total exactly. That headline needs context: 42 of those seats were never in doubt. The real test is the 14 genuine contests, where the record was 11 of 14, or about 8 in 10.

A high overall score mostly reflects how many safe seats a state has. A forecast should be judged on the seats that were actually close, not on the full board. Every seat the forecast got wrong was one it had already flagged as a contest; it did not miss a single seat it was confident about.

5. Did the contested seats decide the outcome?

This decides whether the contested seats are truly where the election is won. Before a single contested seat is counted, BN already holds 37 safe seats, the ones the method was sure about and got right. That alone clears the 29 needed for a majority. BN had won the state on its safe seats before the close races were decided.

So in Johor the 14 contested seats did not decide who governs; they decided the size of the win. BN took 11 of the 14, turning a solid majority into a landslide. They also carried all of the difficulty: the method got 11 of the 14 right, and all three of its wrong calls, Maharani, Puteri Wangsa and Senai, sat here. The lesson for Negeri Sembilan is a warning, not a shortcut. Where the two sides start more evenly, these mixed contested seats stop deciding the margin and start deciding who wins.

6. Did the forecast call the size of each win?

Each dot is a seat: forecast BN share across, actual up. The line marks a perfect forecast.

No. Getting the winner right is not the same as getting the margin right. Across the 56 seats, the forecast of BN's share of the vote was off by about 13 to 14 points on average, and the misses all leaned one way: BN did better than the forecast almost everywhere. The 2026 swing was larger than the earlier one the method had learned from.

Trust the winner, not the exact figure. A forecast can name who wins a seat long before it can say by how much, so any precise margin should be read as a rough guide, not a number.

7. Did the forecast catch the seats that changed hands?

The nine seats that changed hands, and how sure the forecast was that BN would win each.

Out of 56 seats, only nine changed hands between 2022 and 2026, and eight of those flipped to BN. That is what turned a comfortable 2022 win into a 2026 landslide. The forecast caught seven of the nine, and missed two. Eight of these nine were among the 14 seats it had already flagged as contests; only Bukit Kepong was a confident flip call. A seat is forecast to change hands for a plain reason: it was close enough in 2022 that the expected swing carries the leading side's predicted vote across the winning line.

8. What decides a seat?

BN win rate by the community make-up of the seat, from Malay-majority to Chinese-majority.

Each seat's own past vote was the strongest signal, but who lives there came a close second, and it is a two-sided lock, not a story of one community deciding the election. Malay-majority seats lock for BN, Chinese-majority seats for PH, and only the mixed middle is genuinely in play. BN won every seat outside the heavily-Chinese group, and none of the five seats where Chinese voters are the majority.

Who governed was decided by BN's floor of Malay-majority safe seats; the size of the win was decided by the mixed seats and a swing that was largest in Malay-majority seats. The Chinese-majority seats are a small, locked minority, not the kingmaker.

Part 3 · The verdict

9. Can this method forecast Negeri Sembilan?

Short answer: yes as a structural baseline, but not as a confident single prediction, and only after it is retrained on Negeri Sembilan's own data.

What Johor shows: the method called direction well, 53 of 56 seats and about 8 in 10 on the genuinely close seats, and every seat it got wrong was one it had already flagged as close. Where the caution lies: the method leans on the assumption that the recent swing continues. Negeri Sembilan is less predictable on this point, because its 2026 election is a snap poll triggered by a political crisis rather than a normal cycle, and it is a different state with a different community mix, so the model must be retrained on its own history.

Use the method for Negeri Sembilan as a structural baseline that names the likely winners and, above all, flags the contested seats. Present it as ranges and probabilities, never as a confident single call.

Working hypotheses for Negeri Sembilan

The real payoff of this exercise is a set of testable rules about how Malaysian seats behave. Each is backed by the Johor result and should be re-tested on Negeri Sembilan's own data, because it has a larger Indian population and a crisis-driven race. First, Malay-majority seats favour BN: BN won all 29 seats under 30 percent Chinese. Second, Chinese-majority seats favour PH: BN won none of the five seats over 50 percent Chinese. Third, the lock thresholds are about 50 percent Chinese and 65 percent Malay, and a 75 percent super-majority adds no further certainty. Fourth, the mixed 30-to-50 percent seats are the battleground, the only band genuinely in doubt.

Method & data

The winner in each seat was modelled with a multiple logistic regression estimating the probability of a BN win, trained on the 2018-to-2022 transition (outcome: 2022 winner; predictors: 2018 vote share plus 2020 census measures of ethnic composition, median income and adult-age share) and applied out-of-sample to 2026 using the 2022 vote share. A companion linear regression estimated each seat's vote share to measure margin error, and a no-change baseline assigned each seat to its 2022 winner. Training discrimination was strong and coefficient signs matched domain knowledge. Data is real: candidate-level results are from the Malaysian Election Corpus (MECo), a peer-reviewed archive, and demographics from the 2020 census by state constituency; the 2026 result was cross-checked against two independent published sources. Scope: the forecast is structural, using past results and demographics rather than polling, and Johor provides one out-of-sample test, so this result sets expectations for the method rather than settling them on its own.

Recommendations

For anyone curious about forecasting an election with data, this study points to a handful of simple rules. Forecast seats, not the popular vote: under a first-past-the-post system a modest vote lead can turn into a large seat lead. Always beat the no-change guess, because a model earns its keep mainly on the seats that move. Lean on the last result and who lives there, which explain most of the map. Separate the safe seats from the contests, and trust the model's own confidence, publishing a short list of the close ones to watch. Trust the winner, not the margin. And publish before the vote, treating one election as a hypothesis, not proof.

Conclusion

Judged fairly, the forecast did well on direction and poorly on size. It called 53 of 56 seats and matched BN's seat total, but nearly all of that came from safe seats; on the genuine contests it was right about 8 times in 10, and it could not say by how much anyone would win. Every seat it got wrong was one it had already marked as close, the most reassuring thing in the whole test: the method knows where it is weak.

The wider lesson matches the popular belief only halfway. A data model can call most seats in a Malaysian state election before polling day, because voting runs along steady lines. It is weaker on the margins and the closest races, and one election sets expectations rather than settling them. The Negeri Sembilan forecast, published before the vote, is where the method proves itself.
